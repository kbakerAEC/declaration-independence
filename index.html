01 / The background

How did a protest become a revolution?

By 1776, tensions between Great Britain and its thirteen North American colonies had grown into a war. The Declaration explained why many Patriot leaders believed independence was necessary.

⚖️

Arguments over power

Parliament claimed authority to tax and regulate the colonies. Many colonists argued that their elected assemblies—not a Parliament where they had no representatives—should have a say in colonial taxes.

🫖

Protest and resistance

Events such as the Boston Tea Party in 1773 and Britain's Coercive Acts deepened the conflict. Boycotts, petitions, and political organizing spread.

🪖

War begins

Fighting at Lexington and Concord in April 1775 began the Revolutionary War. By 1776, independence was increasingly debated across the colonies.

Think about it: A disagreement over specific policies became a debate about political authority itself: Who should govern, and what makes a government legitimate?
02 / Key moments

From debate to declaration

The Declaration was one important step in a longer political and military process.

April 1775Fighting begins at Lexington and Concord.
June 1776A committee of five is appointed to prepare a declaration. Thomas Jefferson drafts the initial text.
July 2–4, 1776Congress approves independence on July 2 and adopts the Declaration's final text on July 4.
August 2, 1776Delegates begin signing the engrossed parchment copy; additional signatures follow.

Historical note: July 4 is the date Congress adopted the Declaration. The famous parchment copy was signed by delegates over time, beginning on August 2.

03 / Read the ideas

What does the Declaration argue?

The document has several main jobs: explain political principles, list complaints against King George III, and announce that the colonies are independent states.

“We hold these truths to be self-evident, that all men are created equal…”

Declaration of Independence, 1776 — opening of the statement of principles

Natural rights

Rights understood as belonging to people by nature, rather than being gifts from a ruler. The Declaration names “Life, Liberty and the pursuit of Happiness.”

Consent of the governed

Government gets its legitimate power from the people it governs. In this view, political authority is not simply the ruler's personal possession.

The right to change government

If a government repeatedly violates people's rights and fails its purpose, the Declaration argues that people may alter or abolish it and create a new government.

Grievances against the king

The document lists complaints about the Crown's actions, including taxation without consent, interference with colonial self-government, and the use of military force.

A declaration of independence

The final section states that the colonies are—and ought to be—free and independent states, no longer politically connected to the British Crown.

Historical perspective: The Declaration's claim that “all men are created equal” expressed an influential ideal, but equality was not applied equally in 1776. Slavery continued, women lacked equal political rights, and Indigenous peoples faced dispossession. The gap between the ideal and reality became a major issue in later American history.
04 / People and process

Who created the document?

The Committee of Five

Thomas Jefferson, John Adams, Benjamin Franklin, Roger Sherman, and Robert R. Livingston were appointed to draft a declaration. Jefferson wrote the initial draft; Congress debated and revised it.

The Continental Congress

Delegates representing the colonies debated independence and approved the final declaration. John Hancock, president of Congress, signed the parchment prominently; 56 delegates eventually signed it.

The Declaration was not a constitution. It announced independence and explained the reasons and principles behind it; the U.S. Constitution, written later, established the structure of the federal government.
